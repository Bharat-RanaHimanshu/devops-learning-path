Part 1

Why DevOps exists

Every software organization has two jobs that pull against each other. Developers are rewarded for changing the system: new features, fixes, experiments. Operations is rewarded for keeping it stable, and every change is a threat to stability. Put those goals in separate teams with separate managers and you get a wall. Developers throw finished code over it, operations catches it, and when production breaks each side blames the other.

That wall produces a predictable loop. Deploys are painful, so teams deploy less often. Deploying less often means each deploy carries more changes. More changes at once means more things break together, and the cause is harder to find. So deploys become even more painful.

Key idea

The most important idea in this course is batch size. Small, frequent changes are safer than big, rare ones. Automation, containers, infrastructure as code and monitoring all exist to make small, frequent changes cheap and safe.

Part 2

Ship one change, two ways

Follow one change through two organizations. Press Ship a change and watch where the time goes. Run it several times: failures are random, at rates that reflect batch size.

What to notice

  • Most of the traditional lead time is waiting, not working. Queues between teams dominate. Nobody is slow; the system is.
  • The DevOps path isn't faster because people type faster. It removes queues and handoffs, and it replaces days of manual checks with automated ones that finish in minutes.
  • Failure costs differ by orders of magnitude. When the small change fails, there's one suspect and an automatic rollback. When the big batch fails, there are 40 suspects and a weekend of digging.

The four numbers that measure this

Google's DORA research program (DevOps Research and Assessment) has surveyed tens of thousands of practitioners since 2014. It found four metrics that predict how well a team delivers software. The table under the simulator tracks three of them for you.

Speed

Deployment frequency

How often you deploy to production. Strong teams deploy on demand, often several times a day.

Speed

Lead time for changes

Time from a commit to that code running in production. In the simulator, weeks versus hours.

Stability

Change failure rate

The share of deployments that cause a failure needing a fix or rollback.

Stability

Failed deployment recovery time

How long it takes to restore service after a bad deploy. Older reports call it time to restore, or MTTR.

The research's headline finding

Speed and stability are not a trade-off. The best teams are better at both at once, because small batches make both easier. If someone argues for slowing releases down to make them safer, this is the data that says otherwise.

Part 3

What DevOps is, and isn't

DevOps is a way of working in which the people who build a service share responsibility for running it, and use automation and measurement to make changes small, frequent and safe.

It's a tool you install.Tools support it. A team with Kubernetes and a monthly release train hasn't adopted DevOps; it has adopted Kubernetes.
It's a separate DevOps team that deploys for everyone.That rebuilds the wall under a new name. A platform team that builds self-service tooling other teams use on their own is different, and useful.
It means developers do ops work instead of features.The goal is to automate ops work away, so nobody does it by hand at all.
It means moving fast and breaking things.The data says the opposite: frequent, small deploys break things less often and recover faster.

CALMS: the five parts of the practice

A common way to check whether an organization is really doing DevOps, or just using its vocabulary.

C

Culture

Shared ownership of outcomes. Incidents are reviewed without blame, to fix the system rather than the person.

Example: developers join the on-call rotation for their service.
A

Automation

Anything done twice by hand gets scripted: builds, tests, deploys, infrastructure, rollbacks.

Example: every merge triggers the same pipeline.
L

Lean

Small batches, little work in progress, and no waiting in queues. Remove steps that add delay but no value.

Example: replace a weekly approval meeting with automated checks.
M

Measurement

Decisions come from data: DORA metrics, service health, cost.

Example: a dashboard everyone checks after each deploy.
S

Sharing

Knowledge, dashboards, runbooks and lessons from incidents are open to everyone.

Example: postmortems are published to the whole engineering org.

What the job titles mean

"DevOps engineer" on a job posting usually means someone who builds and maintains CI/CD pipelines, cloud infrastructure as code, container platforms and monitoring. Related titles overlap heavily: Platform Engineer (builds the internal tooling other teams self-serve from), Site Reliability Engineer (applies software engineering to reliability, with SLOs and error budgets), Cloud Engineer, and MLOps Engineer (the same skills, applied to training and serving models). This course builds the skills under all of them.

Part 4

The delivery loop

Every change travels the same eight stages. The left half is where software gets made; the right half is where it meets users. Each tool you learn automates or protects one stage. Click a stage, or focus one and use the arrow keys.

The Three Ways

From The DevOps Handbook. They describe what the loop is for.

First Way

Flow

Make the left-to-right path fast and smooth: small batches, automation, no queues.

Second Way

Feedback

Make right-to-left signals fast: tests, monitoring and alerts reach the people who wrote the code within minutes.

Third Way

Continual learning

Use every incident and experiment to improve the system, and make it safe to try things.

Part 5

CI vs Continuous Delivery vs Continuous Deployment

"CI/CD" hides three different promises. The difference is where automation stops and a person takes over. Switch between them.

Watch for this

When someone says "we do CD", ask which one. Many teams that claim Continuous Deployment do Continuous Delivery, and many that claim Continuous Delivery only do CI. Each level contains the one before it.

Part 6

Servers as cattle, not pets

Old-style servers are pets: named, hand-tended, irreplaceable, and nursed back to health when sick. Modern servers are cattle: numbered, identical, built from code, and replaced rather than repaired. The reason is configuration drift. Try the buttons in different orders.

The lesson: a change made by hand is invisible. It isn't reviewed, it isn't recorded, and the next rebuild deletes it. Two rules follow from that.

Immutable infrastructure

Never change a running server by hand. Change the definition (a Dockerfile, a machine image, Terraform), build a new version, and replace the old servers with it.

Infrastructure as Code (IaC)

Every server, network and permission is defined in files in Git, reviewed like code, and able to rebuild the whole environment from nothing.

You'll meet this idea three times: Docker applies it to the application package (Lesson 07), Terraform to cloud infrastructure (Lesson 12), and Kubernetes to running containers, by constantly replacing anything that drifts from the declared state (Lesson 13).

A blind spot worth naming

Kubernetes is the most over-adopted tool on this list. It shows up in job postings everywhere, so teams pick it to build résumés. A three-person team running two services on EKS spends more time on the platform than on the product. Learn it thoroughly (we will), and still default to the simplest option that fits.

Part 9

Where MLOps fits

Machine learning systems follow the same loop with one big difference: three things change between releases instead of one. Switch between the two pipelines.

MLOps adds four things to the DevOps practices you'll learn first:

  • Data and model versioning, so any model can be traced to the exact code, data and settings that produced it (DVC, MLflow; Lesson 21).
  • Training pipelines that run automatically and reproducibly, often on GPUs (Kubeflow, SageMaker Pipelines; Lesson 23).
  • Model serving with its own scaling problems: batching, GPU memory, latency (KServe, vLLM, SageMaker endpoints; Lesson 22).
  • Monitoring of inputs and predictions, not just uptime, so drift triggers retraining before it costs money (Lesson 23).

For applications built on large language models (often called LLMOps), the same ideas apply to prompts, evaluation sets, retrieval indexes, token cost and latency. Lesson 23 covers them.
